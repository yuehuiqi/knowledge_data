Definition / general

  * Segmental colitis associated with diverticulosis (SCAD) is a mucosal based inflammation of the colon that resembles inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) but is confined to the colonic segments containing diverticula in individuals with diverticulosis 

Essential features

  * Localized chronic active colitis affecting colonic segments with diverticulosis, most commonly in the sigmoid colon, with rectal sparing 
  * Mucosal inflammation with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, cryptitis and focal crypt abscesses but no transmural inflammation 
  * Clinical association with diverticulosis, typically in older adults presenting with chronic diarrhea, hematochezia or abdominal pain 

Terminology

  * Segmental colitis associated with diverticula (SCAD) 
  * Diverticular disease associated colitis 

ICD coding

  * ICD-10: [K57.30](https://www.icd10data.com/ICD10CM/Codes/K00-K95/K55-K64/K57-/K57.30) \- diverticulosis of large intestine without perforation or abscess without bleeding 

Epidemiology

  * Prevalence of SCAD in patients with diverticulosis is not yet defined; ranges from 1.9% to 11.4% ([J Clin Med 2022;11:530](35159981)) 
  * Older adults (> 60 years old) 
  * M > F (M:F = 2:1) 

Sites

  * Confined to the interdiverticular mucosa of colonic segment with diverticula, usually in the sigmoid colon 
  * Rectum and proximal colon are always spared 

Pathophysiology

  * SCAD occurs when inflammation develops in areas of the colon with diverticula 
  * This inflammation is often triggered by bacterial overgrowth within the diverticula, leading to irritation and immune response in the surrounding tissue 
  * Formation of fecaliths, which are hardened stool particles, can also contribute to localized inflammation, potentially obstructing the diverticula and causing further irritation 
  * Additionally, ischemia or reduced blood flow to these areas may exacerbate the inflammatory process ([Ann Gastroenterol 2017;30:257](28469355)) 

Etiology

  * Etiology of SCAD is not completely understood 
  * While the condition is often associated with chronic constipation and dietary factors, it can also result from microbial imbalances and a dysfunctional immune response 

Clinical features

  * Signs and symptoms 
    * Lower quadrant cramping pain, diarrhea and rectal bleeding (common) 
    * Fever and weight loss (uncommon) 
    * Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-like extragastrointestinal symptoms (arthropathies and pyoderma gangrenosum) have rarely been reported ([Curr Gastroenterol Rep 2023;25:130](37129830)) 

Diagnosis

  * Multimodality approach with clinicopathologic correlation and exclusion of other differential diagnoses 
  * SCAD and IBD share similar clinical presentations, which can make it difficult to distinguish between them based on symptoms alone; however, imaging studies, colonoscopy and histopathological examination are crucial in differentiating the two 
  * Colonoscopy often shows inflammation around the diverticula, with segmental inflammation in the affected areas 
  * Rectum and proximal colon are always spared, both at endoscopic and histological evaluation ([J Clin Med 2023;12:6084](37763023)) 

Laboratory

  * Normal to mildly increased white blood cell (WBC) count 
  * Increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C reactive protein (CRP) 
  * Negative bacterial and parasitic stool cultures 
  * Negative serological markers (i.e., pANCA, ASCA) 
    * Can help in distinguishing SCAD from IBD 

Radiology description

  * Computed tomography (CT) imaging (contrast enhanced) 
    * Segmental colonic wall thickening localized to areas with diverticulosis (most commonly in the sigmoid colon) ([Cureus 2023;15:e38724](37292528)) 
    * Mucosal hyperenhancement due to inflammation 
    * Relative sparing of the rectum, distinguishing it from other colitides 
    * Significant pericolonic fat stranding, abscesses or fistula formation, characteristic of diverticulitis, are typically absent 
  * CT colonography ([Gastro Hep Adv 2024;3:901](39286621)) 
    * May show mucosal thickening and luminal narrowing without signs of obstruction

Case reports

  * 15 year old girl with lower digestive hemorrhage and abdominal pain ([Front Pediatr 2018;6:168](29922640)) 
  * 72 year old man with bloody stools and nonspecific large bowel thickening ([Cureus 2022;14:e22930](35399418)) 
  * 77 year old man with palpable right lower quadrant abdominal mass ([Cureus 2018;10:e2795](30116674)) 

Treatment

  * Anti-inflammatory drugs, such as sulfazine and mesalazine 
  * Antibiotics (if bacterial overgrowth or infection is suspected) 
  * Corticosteroids as second line treatment 
  * Surgery in refractory cases 
  * Reference: [Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2007;5:27](17234553)

Gross description

  * Mucosal erythema, edema, friability or ulceration of the interdiverticular mucosa 
  * Normal appearances of diverticular orifices 
  * Rectum is typically spared 

Microscopic (histologic) description

  * SCAD resembles IBD in the interdiverticular mucosa ([Clin Gastroenterol Hepatol 2007;5:27](17234553)) 
  * Disease is histologically restricted to the mucosa of the interdiverticular area of the colonic segment that is affected by diverticular disease 
  * Mucosal inflammation 
    * Chronic active colitis with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates in the lamina propria 
    * Crypt architectural distortion (mild to moderate) but typically not as severe as in IBD 
  * Crypt changes 
    * Cryptitis and crypt abscesses may be present 
    * Granulomatous reaction to injured crypts (crypt rupture type granulomas) can be present but well formed, epithelioid granulomas are usually absent ([Am J Surg Pathol 1996;20:94](8540614)) 
  * Epithelial changes 
    * Surface erosions and reactive epithelial changes, including regenerative atypia 
    * Paneth cell metaplasia may be seen 
  * Lamina propria alterations 
    * Basal lymphoplasmacytosis 
    * Focal fibrosis in some cases 
  * Features similar to Crohn's disease, including granulomas and transmural inflammation, can be seen ([Am J Surg Pathol 2000;24:668](10800985)) 
  * Absence of features typical of other colitides 
    * No significant vascular changes (helps exclude ischemic colitis) 
    * No viral inclusions or infectious agents

Brief review of segmental colitis associated with diverticula

Sample pathology report

  * Colon, biopsy: 
    * Moderately active chronic colitis (see comment) 
    * Comment: The histologic findings are consistent with segmental colitis associated with diverticulosis (SCAD), assuming the patient has known diverticular disease and that inflammation is confined to the mucosa between diverticula within the affected colonic segment. Clinical correlation, including assessment of disease distribution and localization, is necessary to exclude ulcerative colitis. 

Differential diagnosis

Practice question #1

[ /colondiverticularcolitisangerilli08.jpg)](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/imgau/colondiverticularcolitisangerilli08.jpg)

A 72 year old man with a history of diverticulosis presents with chronic intermittent hematochezia and mild left lower quadrant abdominal pain. Colonoscopy reveals erythema, friability and shallow erosions in the sigmoid colon, with rectal sparing. Based on this representative image of a sigmoid colon biopsy, what is the most likely diagnosis?   
  
  1. Crohn's disease 
  2. Ischemic colitis 
  3. Segmental colitis associated with diverticula (SCAD) 
  4. Ulcerative colitis 

Practice answer #1

**C**. Segmental colitis associated with diverticula (SCAD) presents as chronic active colitis localized to colonic segments with diverticulosis, most commonly in the sigmoid colon, with rectal sparing. The histology in this case shows chronic active mucosal inflammation, mild crypt distortion and focal cryptitis but lacks granulomas and basal plasmacytosis. Answer A is incorrect because the inflammation is restricted to the segment with diverticula and granulomas are absent, whereas Crohn's disease often shows skip lesions and more commonly has granulomas. Answer D is incorrect because ulcerative colitis is continuous and usually involves the rectum, unlike SCAD, which spares the rectum. Answer B is incorrect because ischemic colitis shows coagulative necrosis and lamina propria hyalinization, which are not seen in SCAD. It also has an acute onset, whereas SCAD is chronic.

Practice question #2

Which of the following statements about segmental colitis associated with diverticula (SCAD) is true?   
  
  1. SCAD always progresses to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) over time 
  2. SCAD is associated with transmural inflammation and fistula formation 
  3. SCAD is characterized by chronic mucosal inflammation limited to segments with diverticulosis 
  4. SCAD typically affects the right colon and spares areas with diverticulosis 

Practice answer #2

**C**. SCAD is characterized by chronic mucosal inflammation limited to segments with diverticulosis. SCAD is a localized inflammatory condition that affects colonic segments with diverticulosis, most commonly in the sigmoid colon, with rectal sparing. Histologically, it shows chronic active colitis, mild crypt distortion and focal cryptitis. Answer B is incorrect because SCAD does not cause transmural inflammation or fistula formation, which are characteristic of Crohn's disease. Answer D is incorrect because SCAD most commonly affects the sigmoid colon, where diverticulosis is prevalent, rather than the right colon. Answer A is incorrect because SCAD is a distinct entity from inflammatory bowel disease and does not inevitably progress to Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis. Most cases respond well to treatment and do not develop into chronic inflammatory bowel disease.
