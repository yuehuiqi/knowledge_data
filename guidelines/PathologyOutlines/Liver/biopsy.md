Definition / general

  * Liver biopsy remains a critical diagnostic and prognostic tool in hepatology and provides essential histological information for diagnosing liver diseases, staging fibrosis, guiding treatment and monitoring disease progression 

Essential features

  * Liver biopsy specimen represents ~1/50,000 of the total liver volume ([Burt: MacSween's Pathology of the Liver, 8th Edition, 2022](https://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/ASIN/0702082287/pathologyoutl-20)) 
  * Liver biopsy changes diagnoses in 6.8% of cases, specifies diagnoses in 8.8% and adds diagnoses in 10.5% of cases, ultimately altering patient management in ~12.1% of cases ([BMC Gastroenterol 2001;1:12](11707153)) 

Indications for liver biopsy

  * 3 primary settings for which histological evaluation of the liver is used ([Curr Opin Gastroenterol 2020;36:184](32097176)) 
    * Evaluation of abnormally elevated liver enzymes 
    * Evaluation and staging of hepatic fibrosis 
    * Evaluation of radiologically undiagnosed focal liver masses 
  * Assessment of disease severity: biopsy allows for staging of fibrosis and activity grading of liver diseases, particularly in chronic hepatitis, hepatitis B and C, metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) 
  * Diagnostic uncertainty: suspected multifactorial injury, overlap syndrome (primary biliary cholangitis / autoimmune hepatitis), metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH) versus autoimmune hepatitis, drug induced liver injury (DILI) 
  * Monitoring posttransplant liver function: rejection versus disease recurrence versus other pathology (biliary / vascular) 
  * Workup of liver lesions with nonclassical / atypical radiological findings and evaluation of radiologically undiagnosed focal liver masses 
  * Copper quantification in Wilson disease 
  * Research applications 

Focal liver lesions

  * Focal liver masses have a wide range of potential differential diagnoses, including benign tumors (e.g., hemangioma, focal nodular hyperplasia), as well as cystic lesions, hepatic abscesses, metastatic tumors and primary malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma 
  * Contrast enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) characterize 80 - 90% of focal liver lesions; biopsy is reserved for indeterminate lesions, with 95% diagnostic yield ([Curr Opin Gastroenterol 2020;36:184](32097176)) 
  * Malignant lesions dominate biopsies of radiologically indeterminate masses, representing 84% of the cases (primary or metastatic) ([Diagnostics (Basel) 2022;12:2079](36140481), [Z Gastroenterol 2023;61:526](36413993)) 
  * In cirrhotic patients, hepatocellular carcinomas account for 70 - 90% of malignant liver lesion cases, while intrahepatic cholangiocarcinomas account for 10 - 20% of primary liver malignancy cases 
  * Metastatic lesions account for 45 - 50% of cases in noncirrhotic patients: most commonly from colorectal, breast or pancreatic primaries 

Types of liver biopsy specimens

  * Percutaneous liver biopsy (PCLB) 
    * Most common method 
    * Approach: intercostal or subcostal, ultrasound guided, especially to target liver lesions 
  * Transjugular liver biopsy (TJLB) 
    * Used in patients with history of coagulopathy, significant ascites, high body mass index (> 40) 
    * Limitation is biopsy fragmentation 
  * Endoscopic ultrasound guided liver biopsy (EUS guided LB) 
    * Recent modality with success rates matching percutaneous liver biopsy 
  * Wedge liver biopsy (intraoperative) 
    * Important to exclude subcapsular hepatic parenchyma in interpretation, since it normally contains fine fibrous extensions from the capsule and may show scarring that is more pronounced than the rest of the biopsy 
  * Percutaneous liver biopsy remains the standard for low risk patients, while transjugular liver biopsy and EUS LB offer safer alternatives in coagulopathy or ascites 
    * EUS LB excels in procedural comfort and diagnostic yield but requires endoscopic expertise 
    * Laparoscopic and wedge biopsies are reserved for surgical contexts 
  * Reference: [Endosc Ultrasound 2023;12:437](38948122)

Specimen adequacy

  * At least 6 - 8 complete portal tracts are needed for diagnosis ([Am J Clin Pathol 2006;125:710](16707372)) 
    * At least 2 out of the 3 portal structures (bile duct, portal vein, hepatic artery) must be identifiable to qualify as a complete portal tract ([Hepatology 1998;28:323](9695993)) 
  * Significantly improved adequacy rates with greater total core length and number of portal tracts can be acquired with a 16 G needle compared to a 18 G needle ([Br J Radiol 2017;90:20170397](28972801)) 
  * Liver biopsy size greatly influences grading and staging of chronic viral hepatitis 
    * Minimum criteria: ≥ 20 - 25 mm long 
    * ≥ 11 complete portal tracts are required for accurate grading of inflammatory activity and staging of fibrosis ([J Hepatol 2003;39:239](12873821)) 
  * Specimen adequacy statement should be included in the surgical pathology report 
  * Quality of the biopsy can be summarized by total core length 
    * Good: > 20 mm 
    * Compromised: 10 - 20 mm 
    * Inadequate: < 10 mm ([Royal College of Pathologists: Cancer Datasets and Tissue Pathways [Accessed 14 July 2025]](https://www.rcpath.org/profession/guidelines/cancer-datasets-and-tissue-pathways.html))

Epidemiology

  * First liver aspiration was performed by Paul Ehrlich in 1883; the first percutaneous liver biopsy for diagnostic purposes was reported in 1923 ([Verh Dtsch Ges Inn Med 1923;35:210](https://scholar.google.com/scholar_lookup?journal=Verh%20Dtsch%20Ges%20Inn%20Med&title=Ueber%20die%20parenchympunktion%20der%20leber&author=A%20Bingel&volume=35&publication_year=1923&pages=210-212&)) 
  * The number of liver biopsies performed for viral hepatitis has declined significantly over the last 2 decades, coinciding with the advent of effective antiviral therapies and noninvasive diagnostic tools ([Dig Dis Sci 2022;67:3366](34129125)) 
  * Although liver cancer incidence in the U.S. nearly tripled from 1975 to 2017, liver biopsy rates did not increase due to improved imaging reducing the need for biopsy ([J Gastrointest Oncol 2023;14:312](36915450)) 
  * Percutaneous liver biopsy complication incidence in the U.S. remains low with major complications reported ~2.4%, supporting a continuing but selective use of the procedure ([Dig Dis Sci 2022;67:3366](34129125))

Diagnosis

  * Major histologic responses to tissue damage or morphologic patterns of injury indicate an etiologic differential diagnosis that can be correlated with clinical findings 
  * Identification of morphologic patterns of injury is a key component of the identification of etiology of liver disease (see [Forms of hepatic injury](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/liverpatternshepaticinjury.html)) 
    * Acute hepatitis pattern 
    * Chronic hepatitis 
    * Granulomatous hepatitis 
    * Steatosis / steatohepatitis 
    * Biliary injury 
    * Chronic cholestasis 
    * Massive / submassive necrosis 
    * Vascular / perfusion injury: inflow vascular; outflow vascular pathology 
    * Sinusoidal pattern 
    * Mixed pattern of injury usually in combined pathology and drug induced liver injury (e.g., chronic hepatitis with steatohepatitis, acute cholestatic hepatitis) 
  * See [Table 2](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/liverbiopsy.html#liverbiopsytable2)

Laboratory

  * Liver biopsy interpretation is done in conjunction with clinical presentation and laboratory findings such as liver biochemical tests, viral serologies, autoimmune markers and metabolic panels; this correlation helps narrow differential diagnoses and confirm specific liver diseases 
  * Liver biochemical tests ([Pan Afr Med J 2009;3:17](21532726), [Clin Liver Dis (Hoboken) 2020;15:153](32395242)) 
  * Screen for liver disease, identify the nature of liver disease (hepatocellular, cholestatic and infiltrative), assess severity and prognosis of liver disease, follow up of the course of liver disease 
  * Tests to assess excretory function 
    * Bilirubin in serum and urine 
    * Urobilinogen in urine and feces 
  * Tests that assess synthetic and metabolic function: serum proteins, albumin, albumin / globulin (A / G) ratio, prothrombin time 
  * Test that assesses detoxification function: plasma ammonia 
  * Tests that assess hepatic injury 
    * Hepatic enzymes: AST / ALT 
    * Cholestatic enzymes: ALP / GGT 
  * Hepatic versus cholestatic pattern on liver function test (LFT) 
    * Ratio (R) to be calculated as (ALT / upper limit of normal [ULN]) divided by (ALP / ULN) 
      * R < 2: cholestatic 
      * R > 5: hepatocellular 
      * 2 ≤ R ≤ 5: mixed 
  * See [Table 3](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/liverbiopsy.html#liverbiopsytable3)

Radiology description

  * Ultrasound (US): first line for liver morphology, steatosis, fibrosis; includes Doppler and elastography for stiffness measurement ([Gut Liver 2025;19:31](39774121)) 
  * Elastography (US and MRI): noninvasive quantification of liver stiffness for fibrosis and cirrhosis monitoring 
  * Interventional imaging: guides liver biopsy and therapeutic procedures with real time visualization 
  * Liver lesions 
    * CT: used for detailed imaging of liver lesions, tumor staging and vascular assessment 
    * MRI: high soft tissue contrast for lesion characterization, fibrosis staging and functional evaluation 
    * Positron emission tomography (PET): assesses metabolic activity of liver tumors and detects metastases 
    * LIRADS is a standardized system for classifying liver lesions in patients at risk for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ([J Hepatocell Carcinoma 2019;6:49](30788336)) 
    * LIRADS categories range from LR1 (definitely benign) to LR5 (definitely HCC), with additional categories for probable malignancy (LRM) and treated lesions (LR treated) 
    * It uses major imaging features like arterial enhancement, lesion size, washout, capsule and growth on CT / MRI to assign categories 
    * LIRADS improves diagnostic consistency, communication and clinical decision making and is endorsed by the American College of Radiology 
  * References: [N Engl J Med 2017;377:756](28834467), [Magn Reson Med 2021;85:2377](33296103)

Microscopic (histologic) description

  * Count portal tracts: 10 portal tracts is considered sufficient for reliable analysis; this is considered a minimum 
  * Assess if subcapsular hepatic parenchyma is present (may show disproportionate scarring) 
  * Document if the specimen is fragmented or intact 
  * Overall architecture evaluation 
    * Examine architecture preservation at low magnification: identify lobular structure 
    * Recognize the 3 zones of the hepatic lobule 
      * Zone 1: periportal, adjacent to portal tracts 
      * Zone 2: midzonal area 
      * Zone 3: pericentral, around central veins 
    * Assess for architectural distortion (bridging fibrosis, bridging necrosis / cirrhosis) 
  * Portal tract assessment 
    * Identify portal tract components: portal vein branch, hepatic artery branch, bile ducts 
    * Evaluate portal inflammation 
      * Type of inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, eosinophils) 
      * Distribution of inflammation (focal, diffuse), severity of inflammation (mild, moderate, severe) 
    * Evaluate bile ducts 
      * Examine bile duct changes: presence or absence of bile ducts; inflammation (cholangitis), ductular proliferation, necrosis, cholestasis, atypia or dysplasia, florid duct lesions 
      * Assess for interface hepatitis / piecemeal necrosis 
    * Evaluate portal vascular changes 
      * Inflammation (endothelialitis in transplant rejection), thrombosis, vascular wall thickening (hepatic arteriolar thickening) 
  * Lobular parenchyma assessment 
    * Hepatocyte evaluation 
      * Assess for degenerative changes: ballooning degeneration, feathery degeneration, Mallory-Denk bodies, steatosis (macrovesicular, microvesicular or mixed), ground glass appearance 
      * Evaluate regenerative changes: increased cell plate thickness, mitotic activity, multinucleation 
      * Look for cytoplasmic inclusions: viral inclusions, iron or copper deposition, glycogen accumulation 
      * Assess for hepatocellular cholestasis 
      * Evaluate for dysplasia or malignant transformation 
    * Necroinflammatory activity 
      * Identify type and distribution of necrosis 
      * Spotty necrosis / lobular inflammation: isolated hepatocyte death 
      * Confluent necrosis (involving clusters of hepatocytes) 
      * Bridging necrosis (zone 3 to zone 1 [centrizonal to portal] or zone 3 to portal tracts) 
      * Massive / submassive necrosis 
      * Zonal necrosis (zone 1 or zone 3) 
      * Characterize inflammatory infiltrate: type of inflammatory cells, distribution, severity, relationship to areas of necrosis, grade necroinflammatory activity (using appropriate scoring systems) 
  * Sinusoidal and vascular assessment 
    * Examine sinusoidal spaces: dilatation, congestion, inflammatory cells, atypical cells, fibrosis 
    * Assess central veins: size and shape, inflammation, fibrosis (perivenular fibrosis), phlebitis 
    * Outflow obstruction changes: evaluate for veno-occlusive disease / sinusoidal obstruction syndrome 
    * Look for hepatoportal sclerosis 
    * Other pathologies: granulomas: location (portal, lobular or both), type (epithelioid, lipogranuloma, fibrin ring), presence of necrosis, giant cells 
    * Identify iron deposition (hemosiderosis / hemochromatosis) 
    * Look for amyloid deposition 
    * Evaluate for nodular regenerative hyperplasia 
    * Search for malignant infiltration: epithelial (primary or metastatic) / hematological / mesenchymal 
  * Fibrosis assessment 
    * Evaluate the distribution pattern of fibrosis: portal based fibrosis / perisinusoidal / pericellular fibrosis / perivenular fibrosis / bridging fibrosis / nodule formation (cirrhosis) 
    * Determine fibrosis stage using appropriate scoring system 
      * Histological activity index (HAI) score / fibrosis stage (in chronic hepatitis) ([Clin Liver Dis (Hoboken) 2021;17:222](33968379), [Burt: MacSween's Pathology of the Liver, 8th Edition, 2022](https://www.amazon.com/exec/obidos/ASIN/0702082287/pathologyoutl-20)) 
      * Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis Clinical Research Network (NASH CRN) grading and staging in steatohepatitis / metabolic syndrome associated fatty liver disease or the steatosis activity fibrosis (SAF) score ([Hepatology 2005;41:1313](15915461), [Hepatology 2012;56:1751](22707395)) 
      * Other fibrosis staging systems: modified Laennec staging, Beijing system ([J Hepatol 2012;57:556](22617153), [Mod Pathol 2018;31:1191](29700417)) 
      * See [Immunohistochemistry & special stains](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/liverbiopsy.html#liverbiopsyimmunostain)
  * See [Table 2](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/liverbiopsy.html#liverbiopsytable2)

Practice question #1

https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/imgau/liverbiopsyBRQ1.jpg

A 54 year old man with a history of recent abdominal surgery presents with fever (39.2 °C), hypotension and jaundice. Laboratory studies reveal leukocytosis (25,000/µL), elevated total bilirubin (6.8 mg/dL) and alkaline phosphatase (320 U/L). A liver biopsy is performed and shows edematous portal tract with dilated peripherally located bile ductules containing inspissated bile and mild portal mononuclear inflammation. No significant bile duct destruction or granulomas are identified. What is the most likely diagnosis?   
  
  1. Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) 
  2. Cholangitis lenta 
  3. Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) 
  4. Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) 

Practice answer #1

**B**. Cholangitis lenta. The histologic findings of ductular cholestasis (dilated peripheral bile ductules with inspissated bile), canalicular cholestasis and mild portal inflammation in the context of sepsis (fever, leukocytosis, hypotension) are classic for cholangitis lenta. This condition is strongly associated with systemic infections and carries a high mortality rate if untreated. Answer C is incorrect because primary biliary cholangitis manifests with florid bile duct lesions, lymphoplasmacytic inflammation and antimitochondrial antibodies, none of which are described here. Answer D is incorrect because this biopsy lacks the hallmark concentric periductal fibrosis and bile duct obliteration seen in primary sclerosing cholangitis, showing instead only ductular cholestasis without significant fibrosis or duct injury. Answer A is incorrect because autoimmune hepatitis would show interface hepatitis and plasma cell rich infiltrates, which are absent in this case.

Practice question #2

A liver biopsy from a patient with suspected Wilson disease shows hepatocytes with pale, granular cytoplasm but no significant copper accumulation on rhodanine stain. Which additional test would best support the diagnosis?   
  
  1. Alpha-1 antitrypsin phenotype 
  2. Antimitochondrial antibody 
  3. IgG levels 
  4. Serum ceruloplasmin level
