Definition / general

  * Infection involving bone 
  * Rare due to use of antibiotics 
  * Usually pyogenic 
  * Classified based on: 
    * Mechanism of infection (hematogenous versus nonhematogenous) 
    * Duration of illness (acute, subacute or chronic) ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Infect Dis Clin North Am 2017;31:325](28483044), [Clin Infect Dis 1997;25:1303](9431368), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380))

Essential features

  * Classified on the basis of duration of illness and mechanism of infection ([Infect Dis Clin North Am 2017;31:325](28483044), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Clin Infect Dis 1997;25:1303](9431368), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380)) 
  * Due to proliferation of bacteria in bone, causing inflammation and necrosis 
  * Most common infectious agents 
    * _Staphylococcus aureus_
    * Coagulase negative staphylococci 
    * Aerobic gram negative bacilli ([Clin Infect Dis 2006;42:57](16323092))
  * Diagnosis is established via culture obtained from biopsy of bone, histopathological findings and clinical / radiographic features ([Clin Infect Dis 2004;39:885](15472838)) 

Terminology

  * Osteomyelitis is infection of bone and bone marrow 
  * Synonym: osteitis 
  * Different classifications are based on: 
    * Duration of the illness 
      * Acute osteomyelitis: present with a symptom duration of a few days or weeks 
      * Sequestra are absent 
      * Chronic osteomyelitis: longstanding infection over months or years 
      * Sequestra are usually present ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Clin Infect Dis 1997;25:1303](9431368), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380))
    * Mechanism of infection 
      * Hematogenous 
      * Direct extension from contiguous site 
      * Direct contamination ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Clin Infect Dis 1997;25:1303](9431368), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380))

ICD coding

  * ICD-10: [M86.9](https://www.icd10data.com/ICD10CM/Codes/M00-M99/M86-M90/M86-/M86.9) \- osteomyelitis, unspecified 

Epidemiology

  * Hematogenous osteomyelitis 
    * Occurs most commonly in children 
      * More than half occurs in children younger than 5 years and one quarter in children younger than 2 years 
      * M > F
    * Among adults: 
      * Most occur in patients > 50 years ([BMC Infect Dis 2010;10:158](20529294)) 
      * M > F
  * Nonhematogenous osteomyelitis 
    * Among younger adults: occurs most frequently in the setting of trauma and related surgery 
    * Among older adults: occurs most frequently as a result of contiguous spread of infection to bone from adjacent soft tissues and joints (diabetic foot wounds or decubitus ulcers) ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833))

Sites

  * Most common sites in children 
    * Areas of rapid growth or increased risk of trauma 
      * Distal and proximal femur 
      * Proximal tibia and humerus 
      * Distal radius ([Infect Dis Clin North Am 2005;19:787](16297732), [Adolesc Med State Art Rev 2007;18:79](18605392), [J Med Assoc Thai 2011;94:S209](22043778))
  * Most common sites for hematogenous osteomyelitis in adults 
    * Vertebrae ([Clin Infect Dis 2015;61:e26](26229122)) 
    * Flat bones of the axial skeleton (sternoclavicular and pelvic bones)
  * Contiguous osteomyelitis 
    * Occurs in sites predisposed to pressure related skin ulcerations 
      * Sacrum 
      * Buttock 
      * Hips 
      * Heels ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833))

Pathophysiology

  * Bacteria proliferate in bone, cause inflammation and necrosis 
  * Spread along haversian system or medullary cavity within shaft and to periosteum 
  * Subperiosteal abscesses impair blood supply, which causes more necrosis and often draining sinuses 
  * Sequestrum: 
    * Dead piece of bone 
    * Gradually separated from living bone by granulation tissue 
    * May pass through sinus tract 
    * Avascular and dense on Xray 
    * Involucrum: sleeve of living tissue created by periosteum, which is deposited around sequestrum ([Infect Immun 2016;84:2586](27354444))
  * Hematogenous spread: 
    * Most common cause 
    * Usually metaphyseal in children and adults 
    * Usually long tubular bones of children; involvement of flat bones is more common in adults ([Lancet 2004;364:369](15276398), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833)) 
  * Direct extension: 
    * Less common 
    * May be associated with trauma or rarely iatrogenic implantation of infectious material 
    * In elderly, may affect vertebral column 
    * May be associated with systemic urinary tract infection, diabetes (affects small bones in feet) 
    * In younger adults, associated with immunodeficiency or intravenous drug abuse ([Lancet 2004;364:369](15276398), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833))
  * Type of bacteria: 
    * 50% of cases are due to unknown bacteria 
    * 80% of known cases are due to _Staphylococcus aureus_ , which produces receptors to bone matrix components 
    * Hematogenous osteomyelitis 
      * Usually monomicrobial 
      * _Staphylococcus aureus_ is the most common isolated organism ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Clin Podiatr Med Surg 1996;13:701](9026404), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:260](4903106), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:316](4987436))
    * All pediatric age groups 
      * Most common: _Staphylococcus aureus_
      * Next most common: group A streptococci, _Streptococcus pneumoniae, Kingella kingae_ ([Infect Dis Clin North Am 2005;19:787](16297732))
    * Neonatal age groups 
      * _Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus agalactiae, Escherichia coli_ ([Pediatr Infect Dis J 1995;14:1047](8745016), [J Bone Joint Surg Br 1990;72:846](2211769)) 
    * Sickle cell patients 
      * _Salmonella choleraesuis, Salmonella paratyphi B_ and _Salmonella typhimurium_ ([Blood 1995;86:776](7606007), [J Pediatr Orthop 1992;12:534](1613102), [Pediatrics 1998;101:296](9445507), [Int Surg 1998;83:84](9706529))
    * Intravenous drug addicts (affecting clavicle, sternoclavicular joint, spine or pelvis) 
      * _Staphylococcus aureus_ and coagulase negative _Staphylococcus_ ([Clin Orthop Relat Res 2010;468:2107](20174899))
    * Posttraumatic cases: _Pseudomonas_ and mixed bacteria 
    * Other known organisms are _Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas_ and _Klebsiella_
    * Rarely associated with malakoplaia

Etiology

  * Hematogenous spread of infectious agents: 
    * Risk factors 
      * Endocarditis 
      * Indwelling catheters (vascular catheters, cardiovascular devices) 
      * Orthopedic hardware 
      * Injection drug use 
      * Hemodialysis 
      * Sickle cell disease ([BMC Infect Dis 2010;10:158](20529294), [Diagn Microbiol Infect Dis 2017;88:75](28258789))
  * Nonhematogenous spread: 
    * Direct inoculation 
      * Trauma, bite wound, surgery
    * Contiguous spread 
      * Infection to bone from adjacent soft tissues and joints (diabetic foot wounds, vascular disease, decubitus ulcer) ([Lancet 2004;364:369](15276398), [N Engl J Med 1997;336:999](9077380), [N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833))

Clinical features

  * Signs and symptoms: 
    * Gradual onset of symptoms over several days 
    * Dull pain at the involved site 
    * Tenderness, warmth, erythema and swelling 
    * Fever and rigors may also be present ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833)) 

Diagnosis

  * Diagnosis of osteomyelitis is established via culture obtained from biopsy of the involved bone 
  * Histopathology consistent with osteomyelitis in the absence of positive culture data 
  * Typical clinical and radiographic findings together with persistently elevated inflammatory markers in the absence of positive culture and no biopsy interpretation ([Clin Infect Dis 2004;39:885](15472838)) 

Laboratory

  * Leukocytosis on complete blood count 
  * Elevated inflammatory markers: erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C reactive protein (CRP) 
  * Blood cultures are positive in 50 - 60% of cases 
  * Bone aspirate cultures may be positive when blood cultures are negative ([N Engl J Med 1970;282:198](4902833), [Semin Arthritis Rheum 2002;31:271](11836660), [Clin Orthop Relat Res 1991;264:178](1997233)) 

Radiology description

  * Conventional radiography: 
    * Not for early detection of osteomyelitis 
    * Reasonable initial imaging modality with ≥ 2 weeks of clinical symptoms 
    * Soft tissue swelling 
    * Osteopenia 
    * Cortical loss 
    * Bony destruction 
    * Periosteal reaction ([Clin Infect Dis 2004;39:885](15472838), [Arch Intern Med 1994;154:753](8147679), [AJR Am J Roentgenol 1991;157:365](1853823))
  * Magnetic resonance imaging: 
    * High sensitivity and negative predictive value 
    * MRI with no evidence of osteomyelitis after 1 week of clinical signs or symptoms is sufficient for exclusion of osteomyelitis ([Infect Dis Clin North Am 2006;20:789](17118291))
  * Late images show prominent periosteal reaction resembling neoplasm ([Infect Dis Clin North Am 2006;20:789](17118291)) 
  * Sclerosing osteomyelitis of Garré: 
    * Lytic bone destruction surrounded by sclerosis 
    * Chronic disease may resemble malignant bone tumor due to destructive and regenerative bone changes ([J Orthop Surg (Hong Kong) 2019;27:2309499019874704](31554485))

Prognostic factors

  * Chronic osteomyelitis develops in a subset of acute osteomyelitis due to: 
    * Delayed treatment 
    * Inadequate antibiotics 
    * Incomplete surgical debridement of necrotic bone 
    * Weakened host defenses ([EFORT Open Rev 2017;1:128](28461939))

Case reports

  * 28 day old boy with _Salmonella_ osteomyelitis ([Ital J Pediatr 2018;44:28](29478416)) 
  * 6 week old girl with brachial plexus palsy with history of osteomyelitis ([Pediatr Infect Dis J 2017;36:1219](28033242)) 
  * 37 year old man with _Salmonella_ osteomyelitis ([Ulster Med J 2015;84:171](26668420)) 

Treatment

  * Antibiotic therapy 
  * Surgical debridement 
  * References: [Diabetes Care 2015;38:302](25414157), [Diabetologia 2008;51:962](18385975), [Infect Dis Clin North Am 2017;31:325](28483044)

Gross description

  * Varies with patient age: 
    * Infants under age 1 year often have permanent joint and epiphyseal damage sparing metaphysis and diaphysis ([Arch Pediatr Adolesc Med 1995;149:537](7735407)) 
    * Children 1 year and older have opposite changes (sparing of joint, damage to metaphysis) ([J Pediatr Orthop 2000;20:40](10641686), [J Bone Joint Surg Br 2012;94:584](22529075)) 
    * Adults have joint infection and extensive bone involvement
  * Acute disease has pus tracking through bone, periosteal elevation and shell of reactive periosteal bone around necrotic center 
  * Neonates may have considerable subperiosteal spread 
  * Chronic disease is accompanied by prominent periosteal bone formation 

Microscopic (histologic) description

  * Histopathological osteomyelitis diagnostics ([GMS Interdiscip Plast Reconstr Surg DGPW 2014;3:Doc08](26504719)) 

**Patterns of acute osteomyelitis**  

  * Osseous changes 
    * Osteonecrosis: necrotic bone tissue can be identified by the presence of trabeculae with empty osteocyte cavities; this is especially evident with EDTA decalcification 
    * Bone trabeculae exhibit fragmentation and irregular contours 
    * May be fractured and completely necrotic, forming bone sequestrum 
    * Granulocyte infiltrate and fibrin exudate can be observed within the medullary cavity 
    * In bone tissue with a hematopoietic function (e.g., axial skeleton), there is a noticeable reduction or complete absence of hematopoiesis 
  * Soft tissue changes 
    * Soft tissue necrosis: characterized by apoptosis, tissue eosinophilia, fibrin exudate and a confining tissue texture 
  * Pattern of inflammatory infiltrate 
    * Neutrophilic granulocyte infiltrate: segmented neutrophilic granulocytes arranged in diffuse and grouped deposits (i.e., microabscesses, comprising ≥ 5 granulocytes) within medullary spaces that are usually highly edematous 
    * Neutrophilic granulocytes are PAS positive (cytoplasmic, coarsely granular) and demonstrate chromatin texture that is plump and pyknotic, indicative of granulocyte apoptosis with pathogen phagocytosis and NETosis 
    * Immunohistochemistry shows specific intense, coarsely granular, predominantly cytoplasmic positivity for CD15 
    * Osteoclasts are also frequently seen on the irregular trabecular surface with neutrophilic granulocytes

**Patterns of chronic osteomyelitis**  

  * Osseous changes 
    * Bone neogenesis: new spongy bone forms with reactive network bone neogenesis, characterized by irregular fibril orientation under polarized light; osteoblasts line these new bone surfaces 
    * Medullary space fibrosis with dilated / ectatic sinus 
    * Fibrosis and formation of granulation tissue is seen in the medullary space 
    * Inflammatory infiltrate comprises macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells and occasional neutrophilic granulocytes 
  * Soft tissue changes: fibrosis and formation of granulation tissue with infiltrate that consists of macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells and sparse neutrophilic granulocytes 
  * Pattern of inflammatory infiltrate 
    * Lymphocyte / macrophage / plasma cell infiltrate: in heavily fibrosed medullary spaces, the infiltrate is rich in lymphocytes and macrophages and can be rich in plasma cells; this infiltrate may appear focal or diffusely inflammatory and may contain a few neutrophilic granulocytes

  * Neutrophils may persist for weeks alongside lymphocytes and plasma cells with bone necrosis and reactive new bone formation 
  * Capillary proliferation and fibrosis are common 
  * Subtypes include plasma cell osteomyelitis and xanthogranulomatous osteomyelitis (characterized by abundant foamy macrophages) 
  * Inflammatory tissue replaces bone marrow 
  * _Salmonella_ infection may give rise to tuberculoid granules with varying degrees of central necrosis ([Am J Surg Pathol 1985;9:531](4091185)) 
  * Bone resorption by osteoblasts results in a bitten bone appearance (i.e., chewed, scalloped bone) 
  * Features of bone necrosis, vascular injury, thrombosis, marrow infarction and dirty marrow are also noted ([J Foot Ankle Surg 2020;59:75](31753572), [GMS Interdiscip Plast Reconstr Surg DGPW 2014;3:Doc08](26504719))

Practice question #1

[ /bonebacterialosteomyelitisWick05.jpg)](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/imgau/bonebacterialosteomyelitisWick05.jpg)

Hematogenous osteomyelitis is usually   
  
  1. Monomicrobial and most common organism involved is _Staphylococcus aureus_
  2. Monomicrobial and most common organism involved is _Streptococcus pyogenes_
  3. Polymicrobial and most common organism involved is _Staphylococcus aureus_
  4. Polymicrobial and most common organism involved is _Streptococcus agalactiae_
  5. Polymicrobial and most common organism involved is _Streptococcus pyogenes_

Practice answer #1

**A**. Monomicrobial and most common organism involved is _Staphylococcus aureus_. Eighty percent of known cases of osteomyelitis are due to _Staphylococcus aureus_ and are usually monomicrobial. Answer B is incorrect as _Streptococcus pyogenes_ can cause osteomyelitis however it is far less common than _S. aureus_ and is more commonly seen in trauma or skin infections that secondarily spread to bone. Answer C is incorrect because polymicrobial infections are uncommon in osteomyelitis but may occur with nonhematogenous forms (like contiguous spread osteomyelitis) and can involve multiple bacterial species. Answer D is incorrect because osteomyelitis is more commonly monomicrobial and less likely to involve _Streptococcus agalactiae._ Answer E is incorrect as _Streptococcus pyogenes_ is not a leading cause of osteomyelitis and polymicrobial infections are less likely.

Practice question #2

Most common microbial etiology of bacterial osteomyelitis in neonatal age group is   
  
  1. _Salmonella typhi_ , _Staphylococcus aureus_ , _Streptococcus agalactiae_
  2. _Staphylococcus aureus_ , _Klebsiella_ , _Streptococcus pneumoniae_
  3. _Staphylococcus aureus_ , _Pseudomonas aeruginosa_ , _Escherichia coli_
  4. _Staphylococcus aureus_ , _Streptococcus agalactiae_ , _Escherichia coli_

Practice answer #2

**D**. _Staphylococcus aureus_ , _Streptococcus agalactiae_ , _Escherichia coli_ , _Staphylococcus aureus._ These three bacteria are all common pathogens that cause osteomyelitis in neonates. Answer A is incorrect because _Salmonella typhi_ is not a typical cause of osteomyelitis in neonates. Answer B is incorrect because _Streptococcus pneumoniae_ is not a common pathogen in neonates; it is more relevant in older infants and children with respiratory or systemic infections. Answer C is incorrect because _Pseudomonas aeruginosa_ is not commonly associated with neonatal osteomyelitis and is more associated with post-traumatic or puncture wound-related osteomyelitis.
