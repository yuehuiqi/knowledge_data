Definition / general

  * Histoplasmosis is caused by _Histoplasma capsulatum_ , a dimorphic fungus endemic to the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys 
  * Clinical spectrum ranges from being asymptomatic to mild disease or severe, disseminated illness, particularly in immunocompromised hosts 

Essential features

  * Infection typically occurs through inhalation of fungal spores from contaminated soil or bird / bat droppings ([Emerg Infect Dis 2016;22:370](26890817), [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
  * Severity of disease corresponds to the number of conidia inhaled ([J Bras Pneumol 2009;35:1145](20011851), [Med Mycol 2021 Jan 13 [Epub ahead of print]](33443574)) 
  * Endemic to central and eastern United States or Ohio and Mississippi River Valleys ([Emerg Infect Dis 2016;22:370](26890817), [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137), [J Bras Pneumol 2009;35:1145](20011851)) 
  * Can present as acute, subacute or chronic pulmonary infection ([Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
  * Frequently asymptomatic in immunocompetent hosts ([Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
  * Progressive disseminated histoplasmosis is a life threatening complication primarily affecting immunosuppressed individuals ([Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137), [Med Mycol 2021 Jan 13 [Epub ahead of print]](33443574)) 

Terminology

  * Histoplasmosis 
  * Pulmonary histoplasmosis 
  * _Histoplasma_ pneumonia 
  * Disseminated histoplasmosis 
  * _Histoplasma capsulatum_ infection 

ICD coding

  * ICD-11: [1F2A.0](https://icd.who.int/browse/2024-01/mms/en#1553838370) \- pulmonary histoplasmosis capsulati 

Epidemiology

  * Incidence 
    * Up to 80% exposure in endemic areas ([Semin Respir Crit Care Med 2020;41:13](32000281), [Clin Microbiol Rev 2007;20:115](17223625)) 
    * Most common endemic mycosis in the United States ([Semin Respir Crit Care Med 2015;36:729](26398539)) 
  * Risk factors 
    * Immunocompromised patients at higher risk for severe disease ([Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137), [Med Mycol 2021 Jan 13 [Epub ahead of print]](33443574)) 
    * Environmental exposure (spelunking, bird / bat droppings) ([Emerg Infect Dis 2016;22:370](26890817)) 

Sites

  * Most commonly, lung parenchyma ([Clin Microbiol Rev 2007;20:115](17223625), [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
  * May also affect bronchi ([Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
  * In immunocompromised patients, may disseminate to liver, spleen, bone marrow, lymph nodes and central nervous system ([Int J STD AIDS 2022;33:544](35343333), [Clin Infect Dis 1995;21:S108](8547497)) 

Pathophysiology

  * Pathogenesis is not fully understood but involves both innate and adaptive immunity 
  * Inhaled microconidia convert to yeast in the lungs 
  * Yeast inhibiting phagolysosome fusion helps with intracellular survival 
  * Elicits granulomatous inflammation, often with caseating necrosis 
  * Controlled by Th1 mediated immunity and macrophage activation (including heat shock proteins) 
  * Infection is typically contained in immunocompetent hosts; impaired cellular immunity permits dissemination 
  * References: [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137), [Semin Respir Crit Care Med 2020;41:13](32000281)

Etiology

  * Caused by the fungus _Histoplasma capsulatum_
  * Transmission via inhalation of spores from contaminated soil, bird or bat droppings 
  * Grows as a mold in the environment and converts to a yeast form in the human body 
  * Thrives in moist, warm climates, especially near rivers and caves 
  * Endemic to central and eastern United States or Ohio and Mississippi River Valleys 
  * There have been reports of histoplasmosis in Brazil, Argentina, India, South Africa, West Africa, Central and South America, Oceania and northern sub-Saharan Africa 
  * References: [Emerg Infect Dis 2016;22:370](26890817), [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137), [Emerg Infect Dis 2018;24:1835](30226187), [Curr Trop Med Rep 2015;2:70](26279969)

Clinical features

  * Immunocompetent patients ([N Engl J Med 2024;390:536](38324487), [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)) 
    * Often asymptomatic or mild, self limited illness 
    * Acute: flu-like symptoms such as fever, dry cough, chest pain, fatigue, hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy 
    * Chronic: productive cough, weight loss, night sweats, upper lobe cavitary lesions; may develop granulomas in lungs, liver, spleen, adrenals, lymph nodes or meninges 
  * Immunocompromised patients ([Clin Microbiol Rev 2007;20:115](17223625)) 
    * At risk for progressive disseminated histoplasmosis 
    * Symptoms include fever, weight loss, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, mucosal ulcers and adrenal insufficiency 

Diagnosis

  * Biopsy 
    * Granulomatous inflammation, necrotizing and nonnecrotizing 
    * Intracellular oval yeast (2 - 5 μm) 
  * Clinical evaluation 
    * Detailed history (travel, occupational or recreational exposure) 
    * Consider in patients with persistent pulmonary symptoms and abnormal chest imaging 
  * Imaging 
    * Chest Xray or high resolution CT to identify lung infiltrates, granulomas, cavitary lesions or miliary nodules 
  * Reference: [N Engl J Med 2024;390:536](38324487)

Laboratory

  * Urine and serum antigen detection 
    * Can detect in acute infection 
    * Can be a marker for treatment response 
  * Serologic testing for _Histoplasma_ antibodies 
    * Recommended for subacute or chronic infection 
  * Fungal culture 
    * Historically, fungal cultures were used before antigen or serologic testing was available 
  * Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) based diagnostics 
    * Currently in development 
    * Not FDA approved for clinical use 
  * Reference: [J Clin Microbiol 2017;55:1612](28275076)

Radiology description

  * Histoplasmoma 
    * Solitary pulmonary nodule 
    * Often have target-like central and lamellated calcification 
    * May mimic malignancy 
  * Consolidations 
    * Patchy or confluent consolidation is possible 
    * Diffuse, bilateral reticulonodular infiltrates are possible 
    * May mimic atypical pneumonia 
  * Miliary histoplasmosis 
    * Multiple, small nodules 
    * Seen in disseminated disease 
    * May mimic miliary tuberculosis 
  * Fibrosing / sclerosing mediastinitis 
    * Dense, infiltrative mediastinal soft tissue fibrosis and inflammation due to exaggerated host immune response 
    * Can encase and compress the airways, esophagus and great vessels 
  * Mediastinal granuloma 
    * Organized, granulomatous inflammation of lymph nodes 
    * Appears as a focal, well marginalized mediastinal mass 
  * Bronchiectasis / broncholithiasis 
    * May occur due to calcifications of lymph nodes (broncholiths) obstructing or eroding into a bronchus 
  * References: [Radiology 1996;199:297](8668768), [N Engl J Med 2024;390:536](38324487), [Lung 2013;191:655](24043490)

Prognostic factors

  * Favorable 
    * Immunocompetent status 
    * Early treatment 
  * Not favorable 
    * Immunosuppression (especially acquired immunodeficiency syndrome [AIDS], transplant recipients) 
    * Disseminated disease 
    * Delay in treatment 
  * Reference: [Mycopathologia 2021;186:697](34498137)

Case reports

  * 40 year old man with a cavitary lung lesion and pleural mass was diagnosed with histoplasmosis following surgical biopsy ([Am J Case Rep 2020;21:e921274](32449900)) 
  * 55 year old woman with severe pulmonary histoplasmosis developed right ventricular nonvalvular endocarditis ([Int J Surg Pathol 2024;32:565](37424325)) 
  * 65 year old woman in South Korea was diagnosed with chronic cavitary pulmonary histoplasmosis after a CT guided lung biopsy revealed fungal infection ([Yonsei Med J 2015;56:871](25837199)) 

Treatment

  * Mild disease 
    * Itraconazole for 6 - 12 weeks if symptomatic ([Clin Microbiol Rev 2007;20:115](17223625)) 
  * Severe acute or chronic pulmonary 
    * Liposomal amphotericin B for 1 - 2 weeks 
    * Followed by itraconazole for 12+ weeks ([N Engl J Med 2024;390:536](38324487)) 
  * Disseminated 
    * Liposomal amphotericin B for 1 - 2 weeks 
    * Followed by itraconazole for at least 12 months ([N Engl J Med 2024;390:536](38324487))

Gross description

  * Histoplasmoma: well circumscribed, firm solitary nodule 
    * Isolated necrotizing granuloma (histoplasmoma) is typically a 1 - 3 cm nodule 
  * Gross appearance varies depending on the pattern of involvement 
    * Pneumonic involvement: areas of consolidation 
    * Multiple granulomas: scattered areas of firm nodularity, with or without consolidation 
    * Miliary involvement: multiple small, gritty, calcified nodules 
  * Cut surface may show central necrosis associated with hyalinizing fibrosis 
  * Concentric rings of fibrosis and calcification may be seen in healed lesions 
  * Reference: [Chest 2013;143:1795](23732591)

Microscopic (histologic) description

  * Usual histologic description of histoplasmosis 
    * Yeasts ([Am J Surg Pathol 2010;34:541](20351490), [Clin Chest Med 2017;38:403](28797485)) 
      * Small (2 - 5 μm in diameter) 
      * Oval shaped 
      * Narrow based budding 
      * Acorn shaped nucleus 
      * Intracellular (within histiocytes) 
    * Granulomas ([Am J Surg Pathol 2010;34:541](20351490), [Clin Chest Med 2017;38:403](28797485)) 
      * Typically necrotizing but may also be nonnecrotizing 
      * May not form in immunocompromised individuals 
      * Typically in the interstitium but airspace granulomas are also possible 
    * Vasculitis ([Am J Surg Pathol 1980;4:13](7361992), [Am J Surg Pathol 2010;34:541](20351490), [Clin Chest Med 2017;38:403](28797485)) 
      * Small vessel vasculitis with fibrinoid necrosis 
  * Unusual histologic manifestations of histoplasmosis 
    * Spindle cell pseudotumor ([Am J Clin Pathol 2011;136:410](21846916)) 
      * Mimics sarcoma 
      * Composed of bland spindle cells 
      * GMS stain reveals scattered _Histoplasma_ within spindled areas 
      * May lack granulomas 
    * Pulmonary hyalinized granuloma ([Chest 1991;100:1720](1720371)) 
      * Dense collagen nodules from latent infection 
      * Mimics neoplasm on imaging and histology 
    * Fibrosing / sclerosing mediastinitis ([Radiology 1977;125:305](910039)) 
      * Dense fibrosis with variable amounts of chronic inflammation infiltrating around and compressing mediastinal structures 
      * Late complication of prior infection 
      * Can lead to intraparenchymal venous infarcts and interstitial changes mimicking interstitial pneumonias

Sample pathology report

  * Lung, left upper and lower lobes, wedge biopsy: 
    * Necrotizing granulomatous inflammation with fungal organisms consistent with _Histoplasma capsulatum_ (see comment) 
    * Comment: GMS stain highlights small, narrow budding yeasts within areas of necrosis, consistent with _Histoplasma_. An AFB stain is negative. Findings support pulmonary histoplasmosis. 

Differential diagnosis

  * In immunocompetent patients 
    * Bacterial causes of [community acquired pneumonia](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/lungnontumorinfectionsgeneral.html): 
      * Acute onset, unlike the more indolent course of histoplasmosis 
      * May have lobar consolidation on imaging 
    * Viral causes of [community acquired pneumonia](https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/lungnontumorinfectionsgeneral.html): 
      * May mimic flu-like symptoms of acute histoplasmosis but lack granulomas or yeast forms on biopsy 
Practice question #1

A 45 year old man with poorly controlled human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) presents with fever, weight loss and abdominal pain. Physical examination reveals hepatosplenomegaly and laboratory findings show pancytopenia. A chest Xray reveals miliary nodules. Urine antigen testing is positive for _Histoplasma capsulatum_. The patient's infection is most likely to have been acquired in which setting?   
  
  1. Contact with cat litter 
  2. Drinking unpasteurized milk 
  3. Eating undercooked meat 
  4. Spelunking 
  5. Swimming in a lake 

Practice answer #1

**D**. Spelunking. Caves often contain bat or bird droppings. Spelunking, therefore, is a risk factor for diseases associated with this exposure. Histoplasmosis is classically associated with exposure to bat or bird droppings or spelunking in caves. Answers A, B, C and E are incorrect because a histoplasmosis infection is not typically acquired by contact with cat litter, drinking unpasteurized milk, eating undercooked meat or swimming in a lake.

Practice question #2

https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/imgau/lungnontumorhistoplasmaTazelaar003.jpeg

A 55 year old man presents with cough, weight loss and hemoptysis after recent travel to St. Louis. Chest CT shows bilateral cavitary nodules. Lung biopsy reveals necrotizing granulomas with vasculitis and infarct-like necrosis. Which of the following findings best supports a diagnosis of histoplasmosis over granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA)?   
  
  1. Cavitary nodules on chest CT 
  2. Palisading histiocytes at the granuloma edge 
  3. Presence of geographic necrosis 
  4. Presence of nonnecrotizing granulomas 
  5. Vasculitis in inflamed parenchyma 

Practice answer #2

**D**. Presence of nonnecrotizing granulomas. The presence of nonnecrotizing granulomatous inflammation supports a diagnosis of histoplasmosis over GPA. Histoplasmosis often presents with both nonnecrotizing granulomatous inflammation and necrotizing inflammation, while GPA does not typically cause nonnecrotizing granulomatous inflammation. Answer A is incorrect because cavitary nodules are seen in both histoplasmosis and GPA. Answer B is incorrect because palisading histiocytes are a nonspecific feature found in both entities. Answer C is incorrect because presence of geographic necrosis is characteristic of GPA. Answer E is incorrect because vasculitis in inflamed parenchyma is common in histoplasmosis but does not rule out GPA.
